Prestige pricing: when a higher price sells more

Buyers use price as evidence of quality. What the research shows, a worked example, and when it fails.

Prestige pricing means deliberately setting a high price to signal quality, exclusivity or status. For some products a higher price doesn't just survive, it makes the product more desirable, because buyers use price as evidence of quality.

What is prestige pricing?

Prestige pricing, also called premium pricing, sets a price well above the category average on purpose. The goal is not to cover higher costs; it's to tell buyers "this is the good one". It usually comes with round prices ($80 rather than $79.99), premium packaging and fewer discounts.

The research: price changes the experience

Wine tastes better when it costs more. In a 2008 study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, people rated the same wine as more pleasant when they thought it was more expensive.

For your store

Cutting the price of a product whose quality is hard to judge can lower how good customers think it is.

Discounts can make products work worse. In the Journal of Marketing Research, people who paid a discounted price for an energy drink solved fewer puzzles than people who paid full price.

Worked example: mid-market vs prestige

A skincare brand launches a serum that costs $8 to make. It is weighing two positions:

The prestige price sells 45% fewer units and earns 57% more gross profit.

How to use it, step by step

  1. Check the category. Is quality hard to judge, and is your product hard to find cheaper elsewhere?
  2. Find the "too cheap" point. A Van Westendorp survey measures it directly.
  3. Match every signal to the price. Packaging, photography, copy and support.
  4. Use round prices. $68 reads as confident; $67.99 reads as a deal.

Key takeaways

  • Prestige pricing sets a deliberately high price to signal quality, exclusivity or status.
  • In the worked example, a $68 serum earns 57% more gross profit than a $29 one.
  • It works when quality is hard to judge; use round prices and discount rarely.

Frequently asked questions

Does a higher price really make products seem better?

Research suggests it can. In a 2008 PNAS study, people rated the same wine as more pleasant when told it was more expensive.

Should prestige brands run sales?

Rarely. Frequent discounts weaken the quality signal and train customers to wait.

When does prestige pricing fail?

For commodity products and anything shoppers can easily find cheaper elsewhere.
